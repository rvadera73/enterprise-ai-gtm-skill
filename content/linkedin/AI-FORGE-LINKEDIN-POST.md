DevSecOps was never fundamentally about speed. NIST's own guidance defines it as security shifted left, shared, and automated — continuous, not periodic. Continuous ATO was never about skipping authorization; DoD's own evaluation criteria define it as real-time evidence replacing a three-year snapshot. CI/CD's own definition is frequent, reliable delivery, without adding to the team's workload.

None of these three ask for speed. They ask for continuity. And continuity is genuinely hard to sustain by hand — a team commits to scanning every commit, and six months into real deadline pressure, quietly reverts to scanning before a release.

AI-Assisted Software Factory's purpose is narrower than "faster DevSecOps." It's giving engineering teams the capacity to actually hold the continuous standard these practices already set. AI Forge is what that looks like, stage by stage:

1. **Prompt** — the compliance baseline gets set once, explicitly, so continuous checks have something stable to be continuous against.
2. **Repo** — a second AI agent reviews every change, every time — the exact discipline that degrades first under pressure when it depends on a human doing it by hand.
3. **Pipeline** — every gate maps to a named control family, and the Compliance Pack is real-time evidence of security posture, generated automatically — literally what cATO's own criteria ask for.
4. **Production** — one deployment config drives deploy and destroy across two clouds; real dispatches caught two genuine defects before either shipped routinely.

The standard was already set. What was missing was the capacity to meet it without it reverting to periodic the moment things got busy. Full sourcing and mechanism, stage by stage, in the article linked below.
